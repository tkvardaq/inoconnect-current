Digital Marketing Strategy:
How to Create One That Drives Revenue

Learn how to create a digital marketing strategy in 8 steps: goals, audience, channels, budget and KPIs. Includes examples, a channel table and mistakes.

Most businesses do not have a digital marketing strategy. They have a collection of tactics: a social account here, an ad campaign there, a blog that gets updated when someone has time. Each piece might be fine. Together they do not add up to predictable growth.

A real digital marketing strategy connects your business goals to specific channels, budgets and numbers. This guide shows you how to build one from scratch, what to include, and how to avoid the mistakes that waste a year of effort.

Quick answer

A digital marketing strategy is a documented plan that links business goals to the online channels, audiences, budget and metrics you will use to reach them. To create one: set goals, define your customer, audit what you have, choose channels, set a budget, plan content, then measure and adjust.

What a Digital Marketing Strategy Is (and Is Not)

A strategy answers four questions: who are we trying to reach, what do we want them to do, where will we reach them, and how will we know it is working?

It is not a list of platforms, a content calendar or a social posting schedule. Those are tactics that sit under the strategy. If you cannot explain why you are on a channel in one sentence tied to a business goal, you are executing tactics without a strategy.

Strategy vs plan

The strategy sets direction for the year: goals, audience, channels and positioning. The plan breaks that into quarterly and monthly actions with owners and deadlines. Build the strategy first. Otherwise the plan just schedules activity.

How to Create a Digital Marketing Strategy in 8 Steps

1. Start with business goals, not marketing goals

"Get more followers" is not a business goal. "Add $400,000 in new annual revenue" is. Work backwards from the revenue target to marketing numbers.

Example (illustrative numbers): A services firm wants $400,000 in new revenue. Average client value is $8,000, so it needs 50 new clients. If 25% of qualified leads close, it needs 200 qualified leads. If 10% of enquiries are qualified, it needs 2,000 enquiries. Now the strategy has real targets for traffic, conversion and budget.

2. Define your ideal customer

Write down who buys, who decides, what problem they are trying to solve, what they search for and what makes them hesitate. Use real data: sales calls, customer emails, reviews, support tickets and your analytics. Two or three tight profiles beat ten vague personas.

3. Audit your current position

Before adding anything, check what you already have.

  • Website: speed, mobile experience, conversion tracking, technical SEO health
  • Search: which keywords you rank for and which pages bring leads
  • Paid: past spend, cost per lead, wasted budget
  • Email and CRM: list size, engagement, lead follow-up speed
  • Competitors: where they advertise, what they publish, what they promise

4. Sharpen your positioning

Why should a buyer pick you over the three other tabs they have open? A strong value proposition is specific: who you serve, what outcome you deliver and why you do it better. Every channel should repeat the same message.

5. Choose channels based on fit

You do not need every channel. Choose the two or three most likely to reach your buyers when they are ready to act, then do them properly.

A common split for a small team is one demand-capture channel (search), one demand-creation channel (social or content) and one owned channel (email). If you are unsure about the search side, our comparison of PPC vs SEO helps you pick the balance.

6. Set a budget you can defend

Two approaches work. Top-down: set aside a share of revenue. Many businesses allocate somewhere in the single digits to low teens as a percentage of revenue, though the right figure depends on growth stage and margins. Bottom-up: take your lead and sales targets and multiply by your cost per lead and cost per acquisition.

Whichever you use, split the budget into media spend, tools, content and people or agency costs. Hold back 10% to 15% for testing, so you can try new things without raiding proven channels.

7. Plan content around the buyer journey

Map content to three stages: awareness (problem), consideration (options) and decision (why you). Most small teams over-invest in awareness and neglect decision content such as comparison pages, case studies, pricing explanations and FAQs, which is where conversions happen.

8. Define KPIs and a review rhythm

Pick a few numbers per stage and review them on a fixed schedule.

Example: A Strategy for a Local Home-Services Company

Example (illustrative numbers): A home-services company wants 30% more booked jobs in 12 months.

  • Goal: 30% more booked jobs, from 100 to 130 per month.
  • Audience: homeowners in three service areas who search when something breaks.
  • Channels: Google Ads for urgent searches, local SEO and reviews for steady demand, email for past customers and seasonal offers.
  • Budget logic: 30 extra jobs at a $120 target cost per booked job means about $3,600 monthly acquisition budget, plus content and tool costs.
  • KPIs: calls and form fills, cost per booked job, review count and rating, repeat-customer rate.
  • Review: weekly call and spend check, monthly strategy review, quarterly budget reallocation.

Notice how every choice traces back to the goal. That traceability is what separates a strategy from a pile of tasks.

How the Strategy Changes by Industry

The eight steps stay the same. The channel mix and the numbers shift with the buying process.

E-commerce

Buyers decide quickly and compare prices. Priorities are product feed quality, paid shopping and search, email and SMS flows for abandoned carts, and reviews. Example (illustrative numbers): A store with a $60 average order and 40% gross margin earns $24 in gross profit per order. If repeat buyers purchase twice a year, you can afford a higher first-order acquisition cost than a one-time purchase model would allow.

B2B lead generation

Example (illustrative numbers): A consultancy wants 12 new clients a year at $20,000 each. With a 20% proposal-to-win rate, it needs 60 proposals. If one in four qualified leads reaches proposal stage, it needs 240 qualified leads, or 20 a month. At a target cost per qualified lead of $250, the monthly acquisition budget is about $5,000. That number now tells you whether search, LinkedIn or outbound can realistically deliver.

SaaS and B2B software

Sales cycles are longer and several people are involved. Priorities are search capture for problem and comparison terms, content that educates, free trials or demos, and lifecycle email. Measure pipeline and payback period, not just signups. Retention matters as much as acquisition, which is why our post on SaaS churn reduction belongs in the plan.

Healthcare and regulated services

Trust and compliance come first. Priorities are local search visibility, clear provider information, reviews, accessible websites and careful advertising that follows platform and legal rules. Check what you may say and track in your market before launching campaigns. See our healthcare patient acquisition playbook.

How to Set Up Measurement Before You Spend

A strategy without measurement is guesswork. Before launching campaigns, do the basics.

  1. Install Google Analytics 4 and define key events, such as form submissions, calls, purchases and demo bookings.
  2. Mark those events as conversions, and assign values where you can.
  3. Use consistent UTM parameters on every campaign link so traffic sources stay clean.
  4. Connect Google Ads and Search Console to GA4.
  5. Pass leads into your CRM with their source, so you can see which channels produce customers, not only enquiries.
  6. Build one simple dashboard with spend, leads, cost per lead and revenue by channel.

Strategy Readiness Checklist

  • Do you have a revenue target broken down into leads or orders?
  • Can you describe your ideal customer in three sentences?
  • Do you know your gross margin and acceptable acquisition cost?
  • Is conversion tracking verified on your site?
  • Does every channel have an owner, a budget and a target?
  • Is there a scheduled monthly review?

Any "no" is your first task.

How do you know if your strategy is working?

Look for movement in the numbers tied to your goal: qualified leads, cost per acquisition and revenue by channel. Give paid channels a few weeks and search content several months before judging, and compare against the targets you set at the start.

Common Digital Marketing Strategy Mistakes

  • Starting with channels. "We need TikTok" is not a strategy. Start with goals and customers.
  • Trying to be everywhere. Spreading a small budget across six channels leaves all of them underpowered.
  • No tracking. Without conversion tracking and a working CRM link, you cannot tell which channel earns revenue.
  • Chasing traffic. Visits without conversions do not pay bills. Plan for conversion from the start; see our guide on how to improve conversion rate.
  • Expecting instant SEO results. Search takes months. Pair it with a faster channel while it builds.
  • Never revisiting the plan. Markets, algorithms and competitors change. Review quarterly.
  • Ignoring sales alignment. If leads are not followed up within hours, even the best strategy leaks revenue.

Step-by-Step Action Plan

  1. Week 1: Write the business goal and convert it into lead and customer targets.
  2. Week 1 to 2: Interview customers and sales staff. Define two or three buyer profiles.
  3. Week 2: Audit your website, tracking, search visibility, paid history and email list.
  4. Week 3: Write a one-sentence positioning statement and test it on five customers.
  5. Week 3: Pick two or three channels and write why each one fits.
  6. Week 4: Build the budget and set KPIs with targets and owners.
  7. Month 2: Launch the first 90-day plan: tracking fixes, core pages, first campaigns.
  8. Every month: Review KPIs, double down on what works, cut what does not.
  9. Every quarter: Revisit goals, budget and channel mix.

Digital Marketing Strategy FAQ

What are the main parts of a digital marketing strategy?

Goals, target audience, positioning, channel selection, budget, content plan and measurement. Together they show who you are reaching, where, with what message and how you will judge results.

How long does it take to build a digital marketing strategy?

Most small and mid-sized businesses can draft one in three to four weeks, including research and audit. Results from the strategy take longer: paid channels show data within weeks, SEO usually within several months.

How much should I spend on digital marketing?

It depends on goals and margins. Work backwards from target customers and your acceptable acquisition cost, then add content, tools and management. Many businesses also benchmark against a percentage of revenue.

Which digital channel should I start with?

Start where buyers already show intent. For most businesses that is search, either paid for speed or organic for longevity. Add social and email once tracking and your landing pages work.

How often should I update my strategy?

Review metrics monthly and the strategy itself every quarter. Do a larger reset once a year, or sooner if your market, pricing or competitors change significantly.

Conclusion

A digital marketing strategy does not need to be long. It needs to be specific: a goal with numbers, a clear customer, a few well-chosen channels, a budget you can justify and metrics you check on a schedule.
