What Is Cost Per Click (CPC)?
Formula, Average CPC and How to Lower It

What is cost per click? Learn the CPC formula, what drives average CPC, why it varies by industry and 8 proven ways to lower CPC without losing leads.

Typical Waste Reduction

You set a budget, launch your ads and watch the clicks roll in. Then you notice each click costs far more than you expected, and a competitor seems to be paying half as much for the same keyword. Why?

Understanding what cost per click is, how it is calculated and what pushes it up or down is the foundation of profitable paid advertising. This guide covers the formula, typical CPC ranges, the factors you control and how to lower CPC without sacrificing lead quality.

Quick answer

Cost per click (CPC) is the amount you pay each time someone clicks your ad. The formula is CPC = total ad cost ÷ number of clicks. If you spend $600 and receive 300 clicks, your CPC is $2.00. Average CPC varies widely by industry, keyword and platform.

What Is Cost Per Click?

Cost per click is a pricing model and a metric. In pay-per-click (PPC) advertising you are charged when someone clicks your ad, not when it is shown. The cost of each of those clicks, on average, is your CPC.

It appears in Google Ads, Microsoft Advertising, Meta, LinkedIn, TikTok and most other ad platforms. It is one of the first numbers you should check, though not the most important, as you will see below.

The CPC Formula

CPC = total cost of clicks ÷ number of clicks

Example (illustrative numbers): A campaign spends $1,500 in a month and records 750 clicks. CPC = 1,500 ÷ 750 = $2.00.

Average CPC vs max CPC

Two terms cause confusion on Google Ads.

  • Max CPC (maximum cost per click): the most you tell the platform you are willing to pay for a click, if you bid manually.
  • Average CPC: what you actually paid per click, on average. It is usually lower than your max bid, because in an auction you generally pay only what is needed to beat the next advertiser.

How the auction sets your price

On Google Ads, your position and price depend on your bid, your ad quality and the expected impact of your assets. Each time a search happens, an auction runs in a fraction of a second. A higher-quality ad can win a better position at a lower price than a competitor bidding more. That is why quality work lowers cost. Read more in our guide to Google Ads Quality Score.

What Is a Typical Average CPC?

There is no single average CPC. Industry benchmarks commonly cite figures from under a dollar to well over $50 for the most competitive keywords, and the numbers change by year and by source. Rather than quote a figure that may be stale, here is how typical CPC behaves across categories.

CategoryTypical CPC levelWhy
Legal, insurance, financeHighOne customer is worth thousands, so advertisers bid aggressively
B2B software and servicesMedium to highHigh deal values, smaller search volumes
Healthcare and home servicesMedium to highUrgent, high-value intent
Ecommerce (general)Low to mediumLower order value, more competition per product
Travel and hospitalityLow to mediumSeasonal swings
Social ads (Meta, TikTok)Generally lower than searchInterruption-based, lower purchase intent per click

Treat these as relative guides. Your actual CPC depends on your keywords, location, device, time of day, and ad quality. Use your own account data as the benchmark.

What Affects Your CPC

  • Competition. More advertisers bidding means higher prices.
  • Keyword intent. "Buy running shoes online" costs more than "history of running shoes" because buyers click it.
  • Quality and relevance. Ads and landing pages that match the search earn better rankings at lower prices.
  • Expected click-through rate. If people click your ad often, platforms reward it.
  • Landing page experience. Fast, relevant, mobile-friendly pages help. See Core Web Vitals and revenue.
  • Targeting. Location, device, audience and schedule all change the auction you enter.
  • Bidding strategy. Automated bidding may pay more for clicks it predicts will convert.
  • Season. Retail, tax and travel spikes raise costs.

Why Low CPC Is Not Always Good CPC

A cheap click that never converts is expensive. What matters is the cost to acquire a customer, so connect CPC to conversion rate.

Cost per conversion = CPC ÷ conversion rate

Example (illustrative numbers): Compare two keywords.

KeywordCPCConversion rateCost per conversion
Broad term A$1.001%$100
Specific term B$4.0010%$40

Term B costs four times as much per click but converts at a fraction of the cost per customer. If you optimise only for low CPC, you would choose the worse keyword. For judging whether those customers are profitable, use our ROAS formula guide.

How to Lower CPC: 8 Practical Tactics

1. Improve Quality Score and ad relevance

Group keywords tightly, so each ad speaks directly to the search. Include the keyword in the headline where it reads naturally. Better relevance can lower your cost per click for the same position.

2. Add negative keywords

Review the search terms report weekly. Exclude irrelevant searches such as "free", "jobs" or "DIY" if you sell a paid service. This cuts wasted clicks quickly.

3. Improve your click-through rate

Write clear benefits, use all available ad assets (sitelinks, callouts, structured snippets) and test headlines. A higher click-through rate signals relevance.

4. Fix the landing page

Match the page to the ad promise, speed it up, and make the next step obvious. This improves quality signals and conversion rate, so effective cost per customer falls even if CPC does not.

5. Narrow targeting where it hurts

Exclude locations you cannot serve, pause weak devices or hours with poor results, and use audience layers to bid down on low-value segments.

6. Target long-tail keywords

Longer, more specific phrases usually have less competition and clearer intent. "Emergency plumber open now downtown" costs less and converts better than "plumber".

7. Review match types and bidding

Broad match without good negatives and conversion data wastes spend. Give automated bidding clean conversion data and a sensible target, and avoid changing it every few days.

8. Pause what does not convert

Cut keywords and ad groups with plenty of spend and no conversions. Reallocate to winners rather than just lowering all bids.

Using CPC to Plan Your Budget

CPC helps you estimate budgets before you launch.

Clicks = budget ÷ CPC. Conversions = clicks × conversion rate.

Example (illustrative numbers): You have $3,000 a month, expect a $3 CPC and a 4% landing page conversion rate. Clicks = 3,000 ÷ 3 = 1,000. Conversions = 1,000 × 0.04 = 40. Cost per conversion = $3,000 ÷ 40 = $75. If a customer is worth $200 in gross profit, that works. If it is worth $60, the maths does not.

CPC Examples for Different Businesses

E-commerce

Example (illustrative numbers): A store sells a $80 product with a 50% gross margin, so each sale earns $40 before ad costs. With a 2.5% conversion rate, the most it can pay per click and still break even is $40 × 0.025 = $1.00. A $0.70 CPC leaves room for profit. A $1.40 CPC loses money unless repeat purchases make up the difference.

B2B lead generation

Example (illustrative numbers): A software company pays $9 per click and converts 6% of visitors into leads, so cost per lead is $9 ÷ 0.06 = $150. If 20% of leads become customers worth $4,000 in first-year gross profit, cost per customer is $750 against $4,000 of profit. A high CPC can be perfectly healthy here.

The rule behind both examples: maximum affordable CPC = profit per conversion × conversion rate.

How to Check CPC in Google Ads and GA4

  1. In Google Ads, open Campaigns and add the columns "Avg. CPC", "Clicks", "Conversions" and "Cost / conv.".
  2. Segment by device, location and hour of day to find where clicks cost most and convert least.
  3. Open the search terms report to see the real queries behind your clicks.
  4. Check "Auction insights" to see who you compete with and your impression share.
  5. In GA4, confirm the same campaigns show sessions and conversions, using the linked Google Ads data.

If the Google Ads click count is higher than GA4 sessions, that is normal. Some clicks do not load the page, and the tools count differently. A very large gap points to slow pages or tracking problems.

Checklist: Is Your CPC a Problem?

  • Is cost per conversion within your allowed limit? If yes, CPC is not the problem.
  • Is your click-through rate below what similar ads in your account achieve?
  • Do search terms include irrelevant queries?
  • Does the landing page match the ad message?
  • Are you paying for clicks in places you cannot serve?

Does a higher CPC mean a worse campaign?

No. CPC reflects competition and intent. A campaign with a high CPC and strong conversion rate can beat a cheap-click campaign that rarely converts. Always compare cost per conversion and profit.

Common Cost Per Click Mistakes

  • Chasing the lowest CPC. Cheap clicks from the wrong audience cost more in the end.
  • Ignoring search terms. Without regular negative keyword reviews, budgets leak.
  • Judging CPC without conversion data. Always read it next to conversion rate and cost per conversion.
  • Setting bids and forgetting them. Auctions change weekly.
  • Sending all traffic to the homepage. A specific landing page lifts relevance and conversions.
  • Cutting budget when CPC rises. Sometimes the answer is better targeting, not less spend.

Step-by-Step Action Plan

  1. Pull 90 days of data and calculate CPC, conversion rate and cost per conversion by campaign.
  2. Calculate your maximum affordable cost per conversion from your margin.
  3. Read the search terms report and add negative keywords.
  4. Split broad ad groups into tighter themes with matching ads.
  5. Test two new headlines per ad group.
  6. Audit landing pages for speed and message match.
  7. Adjust location, device and schedule bids based on results.
  8. Re-check results after two to four weeks and repeat.

Cost Per Click FAQ

What does cost per click mean?

Cost per click means the amount an advertiser pays each time a user clicks an ad. It is calculated as total ad spend divided by total clicks, and it varies by keyword, platform and competition.

What is a good average CPC?

A good CPC is one that still leaves profit after conversion. A $10 click can be great if it converts into a $2,000 customer. Judge CPC against cost per conversion and margin.

Why is my CPC so high?

Common causes are strong competition, broad keywords, low quality or relevance, a weak landing page and poor targeting. Review your search terms, Quality Score and auction insights to find the cause.

Is CPC the same as CPM?

No. CPC is what you pay per click. CPM is what you pay per thousand impressions. Search ads usually run on CPC, while some display and social campaigns are billed per impression.

How do I lower CPC quickly?

Add negative keywords, tighten ad groups, improve ad relevance and cut underperforming keywords. These changes often show results within weeks, while landing page improvements compound over time.

Conclusion

So, what is cost per click in practice? It is the price of attention, and the goal is not the cheapest click but the cheapest profitable customer. Know your formula, track CPC next to conversion rate, and keep tightening relevance.
