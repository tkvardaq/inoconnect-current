ROAS Formula:
How to Calculate ROAS and Know What a Good Number Is

Learn the ROAS formula, how to calculate ROAS, find your break-even ROAS and avoid common mistakes. Worked examples plus a free calculator to try.

Your ad platform says you earned a 5x return. Your bank balance says the business is barely breaking even. Both can be true, and the gap between them usually comes down to one thing: knowing what your ROAS number actually means for your margins.

This guide explains the ROAS formula, how to calculate ROAS correctly, how to find the number you need to hit to make a profit, and the mistakes that make good campaigns look bad (and bad campaigns look good).

Quick answer

The ROAS formula is ROAS = revenue from ads ÷ ad spend. If you spend $2,000 and the ads generate $8,000 in revenue, your ROAS is 4.0, or 4x. To know whether that is profitable, compare it with your break-even ROAS, which is 1 ÷ gross margin.

What ROAS Means

ROAS stands for return on ad spend. It tells you how many dollars of revenue each advertising dollar produces. It is a revenue metric, not a profit metric, which is the root of most confusion.

You can express it as a ratio (4:1), a multiple (4x) or a plain number (4.0). They all mean the same thing: $4 of revenue for every $1 spent.

Because it only needs two numbers, ROAS is quick to calculate and easy to compare across campaigns, channels and weeks. That is why Google Ads, Meta and most other platforms report it by default.

The ROAS Formula, Step by Step

The formula has one line:

ROAS = revenue attributed to ads ÷ ad spend

To calculate it for any campaign, follow these steps.

  1. Pick a time window, such as the last 30 days.
  2. Add up the total ad spend for that campaign in that window.
  3. Add up the revenue that the campaign generated in the same window.
  4. Divide revenue by spend.

Example: calculating ROAS for an online store

Example (illustrative numbers): A store spends $5,000 on Google Shopping in October. The campaign records $17,500 in purchase revenue. ROAS = 17,500 ÷ 5,000 = 3.5. Every dollar of ad spend returned $3.50 in revenue.

Expressing ROAS as a percentage

Some teams report ROAS as a percentage. Multiply the ratio by 100. A ROAS of 3.5 becomes 350%. Confirm which format your platform and your finance team use, because "300%" and "3x" are the same, but "3%" is not.

ROAS vs ROI vs Profit

People swap these terms, but they answer different questions.

MetricFormulaWhat it answersIncludes costs beyond ad spend?
ROASRevenue ÷ ad spendHow much revenue does each ad dollar bring in?No
ROI(Return − cost) ÷ costHow much profit does each dollar invested bring back?Yes, whatever you define as cost
Break-even ROAS1 ÷ gross marginWhat ROAS do I need to avoid losing money?Through cost of goods only
Profit on ad spendGross profit − ad spendWhat did the campaign earn in dollars?Through cost of goods and ad spend

ROI is the broader measure. If you spend $1,000 and get $1,800 in profit back, ROI = (1,800 − 1,000) ÷ 1,000 = 0.8, or 80%. ROAS ignores what the product cost you to make or deliver, so a strong ROAS can sit on top of a negative ROI.

How to Find Your Break-Even ROAS

A "good ROAS" does not exist in isolation. It depends on your gross margin. The break-even formula is:

Break-even ROAS = 1 ÷ gross margin

Gross margin is the share of revenue left after the direct costs of the product, such as materials, manufacturing, packaging and shipping you pay for. A 40% margin is 0.40 in the formula.

Example (illustrative numbers): You sell a product with a 40% gross margin. Break-even ROAS = 1 ÷ 0.40 = 2.5. At a ROAS of 2.5, the ad spend uses up all your gross profit and you make nothing on that sale. Anything above 2.5 is profit before overheads. Anything below it loses money.

Gross marginBreak-even ROASWhat it means

50%2.0More room to scale

Those margins are examples to show the maths. Use your own numbers, and include discounts, returns and payment fees if you can.

Setting a target above break-even

Break-even is the floor, not the goal. You also need to cover overheads, such as salaries, software and agency fees, and leave a profit. Many teams set a target ROAS 20% to 50% above break-even, then adjust once they see actual net profit.

If you want to try your own numbers, the free ROAS calculator works out ROAS and break-even from your spend, revenue and margin.

What Is a Good ROAS?

You will see "4:1 is good" quoted everywhere. Industry benchmarks commonly cite a ratio of around 4:1 as a healthy starting point, and many sources describe typical ranges of roughly 2:1 to 5:1 depending on the industry and channel. Treat these as rough context, not a target.

A 4:1 ROAS on a 20% margin product is a loss (break-even is 5.0). A 2:1 ROAS on a 70% margin product is very profitable (break-even is 1.4). Your margin decides the answer.

Other factors that change what "good" means:

  • Repeat purchases. If customers buy again, you can accept a lower first-order ROAS.
  • Funnel stage. Prospecting campaigns usually show lower ROAS than retargeting campaigns, because retargeting reaches people who already know you.
  • Sales cycle. For B2B with long cycles, revenue may land months after the spend.
  • Brand goals. A launch might deliberately accept a break-even ROAS to build a customer base.

A Full Worked Example

Example (illustrative numbers): A home fitness brand sells a kit for $200 with a 45% gross margin. In one month it runs two campaigns.

CampaignSpendRevenueROASGross profitProfit after ads
Prospecting$6,000$15,0002.5$6,750$750
Retargeting$2,000$12,0006.0$5,400$3,400
Total$8,000$27,0003.4$12,150$4,150

Break-even ROAS = 1 ÷ 0.45 = 2.2. Prospecting at 2.5 barely clears it. Retargeting at 6.0 looks great, but it works largely because prospecting fills the pool of people to retarget. Cutting prospecting to chase a higher blended ROAS would shrink retargeting too. This is the trap of judging campaigns on ROAS alone. How to Improve ROAS

ROAS has two levers: raise revenue per click or lower the cost of each click. In practice that means:

  • Raise conversion rate. Better landing pages, clearer offers and faster load times turn more clicks into sales.
  • Raise average order value. Bundles, upsells and free-shipping thresholds lift revenue per order.
  • Lower cost per click. Better ad relevance and Quality Score reduce what you pay. See our post on Google Ads Quality Score.
  • Cut wasted spend. Add negative keywords, exclude poor placements and pause ad groups that never convert.
  • Improve targeting. Focus budget on audiences and keywords with proven purchase intent.

ROAS for Different Business Models

The formula never changes, but what counts as revenue and what counts as a good result does. Here are two cases that behave very differently.

E-commerce

Revenue is easy to see because each order has a value. The risk is returns, discounts and shipping. Example (illustrative numbers): A store spends $10,000, reports $40,000 in revenue (ROAS 4.0), but 12% of orders are refunded. Net revenue is about $35,200, so true ROAS is 3.5. With a 35% gross margin, break-even is 1 ÷ 0.35 = 2.86. The campaign is still profitable, but the margin of safety is smaller than the platform report suggests.

B2B lead generation

There is no checkout, so revenue arrives weeks or months later, and it has to be linked back to the ad click through your CRM. Example (illustrative numbers): A software consultancy spends $12,000 in a quarter and generates 60 leads. Ten become proposals, and three sign at $15,000 each. Revenue is $45,000, so ROAS = 45,000 ÷ 12,000 = 3.75. If you only counted the first 30 days, you might have seen zero revenue and cancelled a campaign that was working.

For B2B, also track pipeline value by stage. Cost per qualified lead and cost per opportunity help you judge a campaign before deals close.

How to Measure ROAS in Google Ads and GA4

You need conversion values, not just conversion counts. Without a value attached to each purchase or lead, your platform cannot calculate ROAS.

In Google Ads

  1. Set up conversion tracking for purchases or leads, and pass a value for each conversion.
  2. For leads, assign an estimated value, such as average deal value multiplied by your close rate. Update it as you learn more.
  3. In the campaigns view, add the columns "Conversion value" and "Conv. value / cost". The second column is ROAS.
  4. Compare against your break-even number, not a generic benchmark.

In GA4

  1. Mark your key events (such as purchase or generate_lead) as conversions and send a value parameter.
  2. Link Google Ads and import ad cost data for non-Google channels, using consistent UTM tags.
  3. In the acquisition and advertising reports, compare revenue by source and campaign against the cost you imported.

GA4 and the ad platform will not match exactly. They use different attribution models and conversion windows. Choose one source of truth for decisions, usually your store or CRM data, and use the platforms for direction.

Decision Checklist Before You Scale

  • Is your ROAS above break-even after returns, discounts and fees?
  • Does platform revenue roughly reconcile with your actual sales?
  • Have you run the campaign long enough to cover your typical buying delay?
  • Is the audience new or mostly people who would have bought anyway?
  • Will margin hold if you increase the budget and click costs rise?

If you can answer yes to all five, a gradual budget increase is reasonable. If not, fix the weakest point first.

Common ROAS Mistakes

  • Ignoring margin. Chasing a benchmark number without computing break-even for your own products.
  • Trusting platform attribution blindly. Each platform claims credit for conversions, and the totals often exceed actual sales. Compare ad platform revenue with your store or CRM data.
  • Using too short a window. Judging a campaign after two days, before data settles or before delayed purchases arrive.
  • Comparing different funnel stages. A prospecting campaign and a brand search campaign should not share the same target.
  • Forgetting returns and discounts. Revenue before refunds flatters ROAS.
  • Optimising only for ROAS. A very high ROAS often means the campaign is spending very little and missing growth. Profit in total dollars matters too.
  • Leaving out non-ad costs. Creative production, agency fees and tools belong in a full ROI view, even though they are outside the ROAS formula.

Step-by-Step Action Plan

  1. Calculate your gross margin per product or product category, after returns and fees.
  2. Work out break-even ROAS using 1 ÷ gross margin.
  3. Set a target ROAS above break-even that covers overheads and leaves a profit.
  4. Pull 30 to 90 days of data by campaign, and compute ROAS for each.
  5. Reconcile revenue between your ad platforms and your store or CRM so you know how far attribution is off.
  6. Separate prospecting from retargeting and judge each against its own target.
  7. Move budget gradually from campaigns below break-even to campaigns above target, adjusting 10% to 20% at a time.
  8. Review monthly against net profit, not just ROAS.

ROAS Formula FAQ

What is the ROAS formula?

ROAS equals revenue attributed to ads divided by ad spend. If $3,000 of ad spend produces $12,000 in revenue, ROAS is 4.0. It tells you revenue per advertising dollar, not profit.

How do I calculate break-even ROAS?

Divide 1 by your gross margin as a decimal. A 25% margin gives 1 ÷ 0.25 = 4.0. Below that ROAS you lose money on each sale before overheads.

Is a 4:1 ROAS good?

It depends on margin. Benchmarks often cite 4:1 as a reasonable reference, but a business with a 20% margin needs 5:1 to break even, while a 60% margin business is profitable at about 1.7:1.

What is the difference between ROAS and ROI?

ROAS measures revenue against ad spend only. ROI measures profit against total cost, so it accounts for product costs and other expenses. A campaign can have strong ROAS and negative ROI.

Can ROAS be too high?

Yes. A very high ROAS can mean you are only capturing the easiest buyers, such as brand searchers, and leaving growth on the table. Check total profit and new-customer volume alongside it.

Conclusion

The ROAS formula is simple: revenue divided by ad spend. The useful part is what you do next. Work out your break-even ROAS from your margin, set a target above it, and judge each campaign against its own role in the funnel.
